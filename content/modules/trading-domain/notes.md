### Orders and the book

- **Order types:** limit (a price, may rest on the book), market (take whatever is available), stop (becomes active at a trigger price), plus flags such as post-only (cancel rather than take liquidity) and iceberg (show only part of the size).
- **Time in force:** DAY, GTC (good till cancelled), IOC (fill what you can immediately, cancel the rest), FOK (fill completely immediately or cancel entirely).
- **The book:** bids sorted by price descending, asks ascending. The best bid and best offer (BBO) form the top of book; the spread is ask minus bid. Prices move in ticks (the minimum increment); quantities in lots.
- **Data levels:** L1 = top of book; L2 = aggregated quantity per price level (market by price); L3 = every individual order (market by order). Only L3 shows how many orders, and how much quantity, sit ahead of a given order in its level's queue, so only L3 lets you estimate your own queue position.
- **Data structures:** a book has to find an order by ID (cancel, execute), keep each level's orders in arrival order, and find the best level fast. The usual layout is pooled orders, an ID-to-order hash map, an intrusive FIFO list per level with the level's total quantity, and levels in a price-indexed array or sorted vector: see :sec[low-latency-techniques/order-book-implementation-sketch] and :sec[containers/choosing-for-an-order-book].

### Prices as integers

Store prices as integer ticks (or fixed-point integers with a known number of implied decimals) from decoding to order encoding, and never convert through `double`. Most decimal prices such as 100.03 have no exact binary floating-point representation, so two computations of "the same" price can differ in the last bit: equality tests, map keys and level lookups then disagree, and sums depend on the order of addition (:sec[types-and-initialization/floating-point-in-one-paragraph]). Integer ticks compare exactly, hash cheaply and index arrays directly. Convert to a decimal string only at the edges (FIX text, logs, displays).

*Further reading:* [Floating point numbers](https://www.learncpp.com/cpp-tutorial/floating-point-numbers/), [Fixed-width integers and size_t](https://www.learncpp.com/cpp-tutorial/fixed-width-integers-and-size-t/)

### Matching

An incoming order that crosses the spread matches against the opposite side, best price first. Among orders at the same price, most equity venues use **price-time priority** (FIFO); some futures markets use **pro-rata** allocation (proportional to size) or hybrids, which typically give a share to the first order at the level (or the order that set the price) and split the rest pro-rata. Fills happen at the resting order's price. Whatever remains of a limit order rests on the book (unless IOC or FOK). A market order has no price, so it walks the opposite side until it's filled or the side is empty, and it never rests: the remainder is cancelled (some venues reject market orders or cap them with a protection price).

Modifies matter for priority: reducing quantity usually keeps queue position; increasing quantity or changing price loses it (treated as cancel and replace, joining the back of the queue at the new price). The reason is fairness: time priority rewards the moment quantity was committed to a price, so quantity added later must queue behind everyone who committed in between. A reduction only gives up committed quantity and harms no one behind it.

**Self-trade prevention:** venues and firms block an order from matching against a resting order of the same firm or account (a wash trade). Depending on the configured mode, the incoming order, the resting order, or both are cancelled instead of trading.

### Protocols

- **FIX:** tag=value ASCII separated by SOH (0x01), e.g. `8=FIX.4.4|9=...|35=D|...|10=123|`. Common tags: 35 MsgType (D = NewOrderSingle, 8 = ExecutionReport, F = cancel request), 55 Symbol, 54 Side (1 buy, 2 sell), 38 OrderQty, 44 Price, 40 OrdType (1 market, 2 limit), 11 ClOrdID. The session layer handles logon, heartbeats, sequence numbers and resend requests. Flexible but slow to parse.
- **FIX framing:** the header starts with exactly 8 BeginString, 9 BodyLength and 35 MsgType, in that order, and the trailer is the last field, 10 CheckSum. BodyLength counts the bytes from the one after the SOH that ends the `9=` field up to and including the SOH just before `10=`. CheckSum is the sum of every byte from the start of the message up to and including that same SOH, modulo 256, written as exactly three digits (`10=007|`). A parser can split fields with `std::string_view`s into the receive buffer, so nothing is copied or allocated.
- **SBE (Simple Binary Encoding):** fixed-layout little-endian binary messages with schema-defined templates, used by CME's market data (MDP 3.0) and order entry (iLink 3). Fields decode with fixed offsets.
- **NASDAQ ITCH 5.0:** binary, big-endian market-by-order feed. Message types include `A` add order, `F` add with attribution, `E` executed, `C` executed with price, `X` partial cancel, `D` delete, `U` replace and `P` trade. Prices are 4-byte integers with 4 implied decimals; timestamps are 6-byte nanoseconds since midnight. Each type has a fixed length and fixed field offsets: every message starts with the type byte, a 2-byte stock locate, a 2-byte tracking number and the 6-byte timestamp; `A` then carries an 8-byte order reference number, a 1-byte side (`B` or `S`), 4-byte shares, an 8-byte space-padded symbol and the 4-byte price, 36 bytes in all. Delivered over MoldUDP64 (sequenced multicast) with a retransmission server: each packet's header holds a session ID, the sequence number of its first message and a message count, and each message inside is prefixed by a 2-byte length (NASDAQ's sample files use the same length prefix).
- **OUCH:** NASDAQ's binary order entry protocol, over SoupBinTCP.
- **Decoding binary fields:** big-endian means the first byte is the most significant. Read each field by `std::memcpy` into an integer of the field's width, then reverse the bytes with `std::byteswap` (C++23) when `std::endian::native` is little, as it is on x86 and on ARM machines in their usual mode; odd widths such as the 6-byte timestamp are assembled byte by byte with shifts. Never dereference a `reinterpret_cast` pointer into the buffer: fields sit at odd offsets, so the access can be misaligned, and reading bytes through an unrelated type breaks strict aliasing; both are UB (:sec[raw-memory/strict-aliasing], :sec[raw-memory/bytes-on-the-wire]).

*Further reading:* [Introduction to std::string_view](https://www.learncpp.com/cpp-tutorial/introduction-to-stdstring_view/), [Bitwise operators](https://www.learncpp.com/cpp-tutorial/bitwise-operators/), [std::byteswap](https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/numeric/byteswap)

### Feed handling

A feed handler receives packets (A and B lines), arbitrates and orders messages by sequence number, detects gaps, recovers (retransmission request or snapshot plus replay), decodes messages, and updates books. Until a gap is recovered, an order-by-order book can't be trusted; the handler marks the instrument stale.

- **Arbitration:** the A and B lines carry identical packets over separate network paths. Sequence numbers are tracked per channel; for each one the handler takes whichever copy arrives first and drops the other (anything at or below the last applied number is a duplicate).
- **Gaps:** if a message's sequence number is above the next expected one, the messages in between are missing. Buffer everything after the gap and apply nothing past it, because order-by-order messages only make sense in sequence (an execute for an order whose add was lost can't be applied). Request the missing range, and once it arrives apply the buffer in order.
- **Snapshot recovery** (at startup, or when a gap is too old or too large to retransmit): a snapshot states the sequence number it is current as of. Discard the affected book's existing state entirely, load the snapshot, drop buffered messages at or below its sequence number, then apply the rest in order. Orders that disappeared while the handler was disconnected exist only in the old state, so applying a snapshot on top of it leaves ghosts.

### Tick-to-trade

The time from a market data packet arriving at the NIC to the resulting order packet leaving. Components: NIC to user space (kernel stack or kernel bypass, :sec[networking/kernel-bypass-and-timestamps]), decode, book update, strategy decision, pre-trade risk, order encoding, send. Competitive software systems target single-digit microseconds or less; FPGA systems, tens to hundreds of nanoseconds. Software timestamps inside the process miss the receive and send paths, so the true number is measured on the wire, with hardware timestamps on both packets (:sec[measurement/production]); per-stage timestamps from a cheap clock show where the time inside the process goes.

### Pre-trade risk

Every order must pass checks before it reaches the exchange (in the US, SEC Rule 15c3-5 requires brokers to have them): maximum order size and notional, price collars against a reference price (fat-finger protection), position and exposure limits, message rate throttles, self-trade prevention, and a kill switch. They sit on the critical path, so they must be O(1) with no allocation and no locks: keep each instrument's limits and state in flat arrays indexed by a small integer instrument ID, and use integer arithmetic.

- **Notional** is price × quantity, and it outgrows 32 bits quickly: a price of 150.25 with 4 implied decimals is 1,502,500, and 10,000 shares makes 15,025,000,000, far above `INT32_MAX` (about 2.1 × 10⁹). Signed overflow is UB, not a wrapped value you can test afterwards (:sec[types-and-initialization/overflow-division-and-shifts]). Compute in `int64_t` and still guard the multiplication: compare `qty > max_notional / px` before multiplying, or use `__builtin_mul_overflow` (GCC and Clang) or C++26's saturating `std::mul_sat`.
- **Price collar:** reject a buy priced more than a band above the reference (last trade, midpoint or the exchange's reference price) and a sell more than a band below it, with the band precomputed in ticks.
- **Message throttle:** a token bucket allows bursts up to a capacity `B` and a sustained rate `r`. Its whole state is a token count and the time of the last refill. On each order: add `elapsed × r` tokens, capped at `B`; if at least one token is left, take it and accept, otherwise reject. Keep time in integer nanoseconds and tokens in fixed-point integers so nothing drifts, and treat a timestamp earlier than the last one as zero elapsed time (with unsigned arithmetic it would underflow to a huge refill).

### Strategy vocabulary

Market makers quote both sides and earn the spread while managing inventory risk; their main danger is *adverse selection* (being filled by better-informed traders right before the price moves against them). Speed matters for updating or cancelling quotes before being picked off, and for taking stale quotes. Colocation puts servers in the exchange's data center: CME in Aurora, Illinois; NYSE in Mahwah, NJ; NASDAQ in Carteret, NJ.
